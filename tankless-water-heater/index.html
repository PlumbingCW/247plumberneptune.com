A tankless water heater heats water on demand as it flows through a heat exchanger, so there is no stored tank to run out. The appeal is plain: hot water for as long as the unit can keep up, and a smaller footprint on the wall. The catch is that most homes were built for a tank, and the switch can touch the gas line, the vent and the water quality.

Our plumbers install, service and descale tankless units in Neptune. We start with the building, not the brochure, because a poor fit costs more than a good tank would have.

When tankless is a poor fit

Tankless is not right for every house. A home with a small gas line, a long vent run or very low-flow fixtures may struggle. A household that uses hot water in tiny bursts, such as a quick rinse every few minutes, can trigger the unit's minimum flow limit and cause the water to cycle between hot and cold.

It is also a poor choice where nobody will maintain it. Without regular descaling, a heat exchanger in hard water loses efficiency. If you want the lowest-effort option, a quality tank may suit you better. Our water heater replacement page compares the types.

Gas line sizing and the BTU load

A tankless unit can pull a large gas load at once, often far more than a tank burner. If the existing line, meter or regulator cannot deliver that volume, the unit starves and cuts out. We calculate the load of all gas appliances and the length of the run before we recommend a model.

If the line is too small, upsizing it is part of the job. Smell gas at any time and you leave the building and call the gas utility or 911 before calling us. For planning a unit in a new spot, see water heater installation.

Scale and Neptune's 72 to 140 ppm water

New Jersey American Water reports hardness of 72 to 140 ppm in its Coastal North system, which serves Neptune Township and Ocean Grove. That equals about 4 to 8 grains per gallon, and your street may sit anywhere in that moderately hard to hard range.

A tankless heat exchanger has narrow passages, so scale narrows them further and lowers output. The payoff is a maintenance habit: ask for isolation valves at install so descaling takes minutes, and plan a flush on the schedule the manufacturer sets. The same advice applies to homes in Ocean Township and Neptune City, which share the system.

How a descaling flush works

A flush is a closed-loop job done through service ports.

  1. Isolate the unit

    We close the isolation valves and shut off power and gas to the heater.

  2. Connect a pump

    Hoses and a small pump link the service ports to a bucket of descaling solution.

  3. Circulate

    The solution runs through the heat exchanger for the time the maker specifies, dissolving mineral buildup.

  4. Rinse and clean the filter

    We flush with clean water and clear the inlet screen of debris.

  5. Restore and test

    Valves reopen, the unit fires, and we check flow rate, temperature and error codes.

Quirks to know before you buy

Tankless units behave differently from tanks. None of these is a dealbreaker, but each deserves a straight answer.

Cold-water sandwich

You may feel a cold slug between two hot bursts when the unit shuts down and restarts. Short pauses between uses cause it.

Minimum flow

Very low flow can fail to trigger the burner. Aerators and low-flow showerheads can matter.

Wait time at far fixtures

Hot water must still travel through the pipes. A recirculation loop or point-of-use pump shortens the wait.

Simultaneous demand

Two showers and a dishwasher can exceed one unit's output. Size for your busiest hour.

Power for the fan

Gas units still need electricity for ignition and the fan, so a power outage means no hot water.

Site checks before we quote

These are the items we look at during a visit.

  • Gas meter, regulator and pipe size, and the distance to the new location.
  • Vent path through wall or roof, and clearance from windows and openings.
  • Condensate drain location for condensing models.
  • Electrical outlet within reach for the control board and fan.
  • Water pressure and flow at the fixtures you use most.
  • Space for isolation valves and a service-port flush.

Venting upgrades, permits and inspection

Permit rules vary, so ask the Neptune Township construction office what a tankless install requires. The township's 2018 fee schedule lists $27 per fixture or device, including water heaters up to 99 gallons, and the township lists rough, finish and final plumbing inspections for one and two family dwellings. Confirm current fees with the office.

Venting changes are where jobs surprise people. Moving from a chimney flue to a sidewall vent can mean new penetrations and clearance checks. We flag that during the visit so the price you hear includes it.

What about lifespan?

Manufacturers often cite a longer service life for tankless units than for tanks, but the real number depends on water quality, descaling and load. Neptune's moderately hard water makes maintenance the deciding factor. Treat any claim of a specific lifespan as a best case, and ask what the warranty requires you to do.
