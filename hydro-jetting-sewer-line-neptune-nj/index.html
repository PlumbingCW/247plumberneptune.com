Hydro Jetting a Sewer Line in Neptune, NJ: How It Works

What the jetter actually does inside your pipe, what it cannot fix, and how to decide between jetting and a cable machine.

By the Neptune 24/7 Plumbing team · Published October 15, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026

24/7 Emergency Line Open(732) 466-1098Call now!

Hydro jetting clears a sewer line by firing high-pressure water from a nozzle inside the pipe. The water scours grease, scale, sludge and loose roots off the pipe wall and flushes them downstream. A cable machine, by contrast, pokes a hole through a blockage and leaves the rest of the buildup behind.

For a main line that backs up again every few months, jetting is often the better tool, but only if the pipe can take it. A camera look comes first, because a cracked or collapsed section will not be fixed by water. This guide covers how the job works, what separates it from other methods, and what to check before you book it.

What happens inside the pipe

A jetting hose runs from a truck or trailer-mounted pump into your sewer cleanout. The hose ends in a nozzle with several small jets, most pointing backward. Those rear jets pull the hose forward through the line and blast the wall as it goes.

On the way back out, the tech slows down and works the worst stretches again. The debris washes toward the municipal main or septic system, not into your house. Residential jetters run at pressures in the thousands of psi, and the right setting depends on pipe material and condition.

Many jobs combine two steps. The plumber cables a stoppage open, runs a camera, then jets if the footage shows heavy buildup.

How a jetting visit usually runs

The order matters more than the machine. Skipping the first two steps is how a pipe gets damaged.

  1. Locate the cleanout

    The plumber finds the access point, often a capped fitting in the basement, crawl space or yard near the foundation.

  2. Run a camera first

    A small camera on a flexible rod shows pipe material, joints, sags and any break. The footage decides whether jetting is safe.

  3. Set pressure and nozzle

    A gentle nozzle suits clay or cast iron with scale. A cutting nozzle is for roots and is used with care.

  4. Jet in passes

    The hose goes in until it meets resistance, then the tech works forward and back until water runs clean.

  5. Confirm with a second look

    A final camera pass or a flow test shows the wall is clear and the line drains freely.

Signs a main line may need more than a cable

One slow sink is not a sewer problem. Several of these together point to the main line.

Gurgling in other drains

Flush a toilet and hear a bubbling shower drain. Air is being pushed back through the line.

Backups in the lowest fixture

A basement floor drain or tub is usually the first to show a blocked main.

Repeat clogs

If a cable opened it and the same stoppage returned within a season, buildup is likely lining the pipe.

Several fixtures slow at once

Slow drains on every floor suggest the shared line, not a trap under one sink.

Sewer odor outdoors

A smell near the cleanout or yard can mean standing waste in a sagging section.

When jetting is the wrong call

Jetting will not fix a broken, offset or collapsed pipe, and it can make a weak pipe worse. If the camera shows a crushed section, an open joint or a belly full of standing water, the answer is repair, not more cleaning. For those cases, see sewer line cleaning first to open the line safely, then decide on repair with real footage in hand.

What to do before the plumber arrives

A few minutes of prep makes the job faster and safer.

  • Stop running water. Skip showers, laundry and the dishwasher until the line is clear.
  • Clear a path to the cleanout, including stored boxes in the basement.
  • Note which fixtures back up and in what order.
  • Take a photo of any water that has come up through a floor drain, in case you file an insurance claim.
  • Keep children and pets away from the affected floor.
  • Skip chemical drain openers, since they can splash on the tech and hide what the camera sees.

How to keep the line clear afterward

Jetting resets the pipe, but habits decide how long it stays clean. Keep grease out of the kitchen drain, wipe pans with a paper towel and throw cooking fat in the trash. Flushable-labeled wipes do not break down like toilet paper, so bin them.

Roots are the other repeat offender. If the camera found roots entering at a joint, expect them to return, and ask about a repeat jetting schedule. The service pages on hydro jetting in Neptune explain how that works in practice.

What Neptune homeowners should know

Neptune Township has a Sewer Maintenance Department, and Ocean Grove has its own Ocean Grove Sewerage Authority with an office on Corlies Avenue. Which body handles your street decides who to ask about the public main. Your plumber handles the private lateral from the house to the main.

The township says to call the police department for after-hours sewer emergencies. That is useful to know before a backup at night, because a blockage in the public main is not something jetting your own line will fix.

Ocean Grove is a National Register historic district with Victorian wood-frame homes from the 1870s to the 1920s. Older homes there may have older lateral pipe, so a camera look before any jetting is a sensible step. Neighboring towns differ on who runs the sewer. In Neptune City ask the borough, and in Asbury Park ask the city.
