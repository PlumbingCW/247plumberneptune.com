Your water service line is the buried pipe that carries water from the street main, through the curb stop and meter, into your building. When it breaks, you lose pressure or water entirely, or a wet patch opens in the yard with no obvious source. If water is running loose, close the main valve inside the house and call the 24/7 emergency line at (732) 466-1098. Do not dig near the line yourself.

This page covers repair and replacement of that buried line, plus how New Jersey's lead service line law affects your choices. It does not cover the sewer lateral, which is a separate pipe. For that, see sewer line repair.

Yard flooding or the whole house without water?

Close the main valve inside the house first. If the water keeps flowing into the yard, the loss is on the buried side of that valve, between the meter and the building, or in the utility's section before the meter. Your water bill lists a customer service number for reporting a main or curb-side leak.

Keep people and cars away from soft or sinking ground. If water is reaching a basement near outlets or the panel, stay out of the wet area. Call (732) 466-1098 and we will work out which side of the meter the problem sits on and who needs to be involved.

Who owns which part of the line?

Ownership usually splits at a point near the property line or the curb stop, but the boundary differs by utility and by town. The curb stop is the shutoff valve in a small box near the street. The meter and the main sit on the utility side of the story, while pipe on your property is normally yours to maintain.

Because this varies, ask the utility using the customer service number on your water bill before any digging. Belmar is billed through the borough's Water/Sewer Department, so Belmar residents should start there. Getting the boundary in writing prevents a dispute about who pays.

Signs the service line is failing

A service line gives off clues long before it breaks completely.

Pressure drops at every fixture

If the whole house loses force at once, the problem is ahead of the branch piping, not at one faucet.

Wet or soft patch between the street and house

Green grass in a dry week or a sunken strip can mark the path of a leaking line.

Meter turns with the house off

Water use you cannot explain suggests a loss on the buried section. See the meter test under leak detection.

Dirty or sputtering water

Air and sediment enter a damaged line, and sputtering after a quiet period is a hint.

Mud around the meter or curb box

Damp earth near either point can mean a leaking fitting or valve.

Lead service lines and what New Jersey law requires

P.L.2021, c.183, effective July 22, 2021, requires community water systems to replace all lead service lines within 10 years. The law covers the entire line from the main to the building, whether the section is owned by the property or by the water system. If your line has to be opened for a repair, ask the utility whether it is lead before you spend money on a patch.

New Jersey American Water's 2025 Coastal North monitoring found a lead 90th percentile of 2 ppb against a 15 ppb action level. Of 104 homes sampled, 2 were above the action level. The report notes that corrosion of pipes and fittings can put lead and copper into water. Those results describe the system, not your house, so check your own line material.

How to tell what your line is made of

Look where the line enters the house, usually near the meter or main valve. Then run through these checks.

  • Scrape the pipe lightly with a coin. Lead turns shiny silver and is soft enough to dent.
  • Hold a magnet to it. Galvanized steel attracts the magnet, while lead and copper do not.
  • Copper looks orange-brown and scratches to a bright penny color.
  • Gray plastic or blue and black pipe suggests newer material.
  • Photograph the pipe, then ask the utility for its service line inventory entry.
  • Never cut or sand a suspected lead pipe yourself.

We can confirm the material when we expose the line. Replacement of a lead line should cover the full length that the law targets.

A line that has leaked twice, or is made of lead or galvanized steel, is usually replaced as one run rather than patched.

Low pressure is not always the buried line

A clogged aerator, a failed pressure reducing valve or a partly closed main valve can mimic a bad service line. We test pressure at an outdoor hose bib with a gauge before any digging. A stable reading below what the house needs points to the line or the main, while a swing between high and low points to the valve.

Corroded galvanized branches inside the house can also choke flow. In that case the fix is repiping inside the building, not a new service line.

How a service line job runs

Each step is tied to protecting the utility and your property.

  1. Mark-out before digging

    Underground utilities must be marked through the state call-before-you-dig system. Marking comes first, every time.

  2. Shut off at the curb stop

    If the interior main valve does not hold, the curb stop or the utility stops the flow.

  3. Expose and confirm

    We expose the line, confirm the break and read the pipe material.

  4. Replace and connect

    New pipe is connected at the curb stop and at the building, with a shutoff and a check on where it passes through the foundation.

  5. Pressure test and inspect

    The new line is tested under pressure and inspected before the trench is closed.

Permit and inspection in Neptune

Permit rules vary, so ask the Neptune Township construction office what applies to your job. The township's 2018 fee schedule lists $95 to demolish a sewer or water service and $27 per plumbing fixture or device, and you should confirm current fees with the office.

For one and two family dwellings, rough plumbing inspections include the water service and are done before anything is covered. Request them at least 24 hours ahead. The township says it performs them within three business days of your requested time, so do not backfill the trench until the inspector has seen the pipe.
