Hydro jetting is sewer jetting: a hose with a special nozzle sends pressurized water through a drain or sewer line, cutting away grease, scale, silt and root hairs from the entire pipe wall. A cable only pokes a hole through a blockage, while a jet cleans the pipe itself. When a line keeps backing up in your Neptune home, that difference decides whether the fix lasts.

Our 24/7 emergency line, (732) 466-1098, is open around the clock for backups in progress. We camera the pipe first so we know jetting is safe, then jet it, and camera it again.

Why a cable sometimes is not enough

A drain machine cuts through the center of a clog and leaves a coating on the pipe wall. In a kitchen line or an old main, that coating rebuilds into a new blockage, sometimes within weeks. The customer feels like the plumber was just there, because he was.

Jetting removes the coating. Water exits the nozzle in several angled streams, scours the wall as the hose is fed forward, and flushes the debris downstream, with the hose pulling itself through. It is the standard answer for grease-lined lines, heavy scale and the hair-fine roots that grow back along a joint.

What a jet removes from a pipe

Kitchen grease

Cooled fat forms a white, waxy layer that narrows a drain from the wall inward.

Mineral scale

Hard deposits build slowly and give grease a rough surface to grab.

Root hairs

Fine roots enter at joints and thicken. A jet cuts them back along the wall, though they return if the joint stays open.

Silt and sand

Sediment settles in low spots and flows out when the jet pushes it to a larger pipe.

Soap and hair mats

Bathroom branches collect sticky residue a cutting head does not fully scrape off.

Camera, jet, camera: how the job runs

Jetting is not a blind job. The sequence below is why we do it with a camera at both ends.

  1. Look first

    A camera goes in through the cleanout to confirm the pipe is intact and to spot cracks, offsets or sags.

  2. Choose a nozzle

    A forward-facing nozzle bores through a hard plug, while a rear-facing one scours as it is pulled back.

  3. Set the pressure

    We start low and raise it only as the pipe and debris demand.

  4. Jet and retrieve

    We feed the hose downline, pull back slowly and catch what comes out at the cleanout.

  5. Look again

    A second camera pass shows a clean wall, or shows damage the debris was hiding.

Pressure is matched to the pipe. More is not better, and thin or old pipe gets less.

When jetting is the wrong tool

A collapsed or crushed section of pipe cannot be jetted open, and pressure can make a cracked one worse. Old clay or cast iron with broken joints may leak into the soil once the plug is gone. A belly, which is a sag that holds standing water, will keep collecting waste no matter how clean the wall is. In each case we show you the footage and talk about repairing the sewer line instead.

Questions to ask before anyone jets your line

Whoever you hire, these answers tell you whether the job is being done right.

  • Will you run a camera before and after, and will I see the footage?
  • Is there a cleanout, or will you pull a fixture or toilet?
  • What pressure and nozzle will you use on my pipe material?
  • What happens if the camera shows a crack or collapse?
  • Can you contain the backwash so it does not flood the basement?
  • Will you give me the price before the hose goes in?
