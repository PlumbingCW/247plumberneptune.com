Boiler Repair in Neptune, NJ: Signs, Causes and Who to Call

How to read the symptoms, what is safe to check yourself, and where the line falls between plumbing and burner work.

By the Neptune 24/7 Plumbing team · Published October 15, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026

24/7 Emergency Line Open(732) 466-1098Call now!

A boiler that has stopped heating usually fails for one of a few reasons: no fuel or power, low water pressure, a tripped safety control, trapped air or a failed pump. Each has its own clue. A clicking igniter, a pressure gauge near zero, radiators that are warm at the bottom and cold at the top, or a boiler that fires and shuts off within a minute all point in different directions.

This guide explains what those clues mean and which checks are safe for a homeowner. It also draws a line between plumbing and burner work, because some faults need a heating or fuel specialist, and a good plumber will say so.

If you smell gas, leave first

If you smell gas, leave the building right away and do not flip switches or use a phone inside. Call the gas utility or 911 from outside, and call a plumber only after they say it is safe. If a flood has reached the boiler, keep away from wet electrical areas and do not touch panels in standing water.

What the pressure gauge tells you

Most home boilers hold cold pressure around 12 to 15 psi, and the gauge sits on the front or side of the unit. Pressure that drifts toward zero means the system has lost water somewhere. A reading that climbs above about 25 psi when hot can point to a failing expansion tank, since the system has nowhere to store the heated water.

Do not keep adding water if the gauge falls every few days. A leak that is topped up quietly will corrode the boiler from inside. A pressure test or a visual check of joints and radiator valves finds the leak.

Checks you can safely do before calling

Take these steps in order, and stop if anything seems wrong.

  1. Confirm power and thermostat

    Check the boiler switch, the breaker, and that the thermostat is set to heat and above room temperature.

  2. Read the pressure gauge

    With the boiler cold, note the reading. Below about 10 psi, low pressure may be stopping the burner from firing.

  3. Look for water

    Check the floor, the relief valve discharge pipe and the pump flanges for drips or rust trails.

  4. Bleed one radiator

    Open the bleed valve a quarter turn with a key and close it when water appears. Re-check pressure after.

  5. Reset once

    If your unit has a reset button, press it once. If it locks out again, the fault is real and repeated resets can flood the chamber with fuel.

  6. Photograph the fault code

    Many boilers show a number or light pattern. A photo helps the technician arrive with the right part.

Common causes of repeat breakdowns

These keep returning when the root cause is not fixed.

Scale and sludge

Mineral deposits and rust slow heat transfer and cause noise. A system flush helps, and a water test shows how bad it is.

Failed circulator pump

The boiler heats but water does not move. Radiators stay cold even though the boiler is hot.

Faulty zone valve

One zone stays cold while others heat. The valve motor or the head has failed.

Bad expansion tank

Pressure spikes and the relief valve drips. The tank has lost its air charge or is waterlogged.

Blocked flue or vent

The boiler shuts off for safety. Do not bypass it. Have the venting inspected.

Repair, replace or upgrade: how to decide

A single failed part, such as a pump, valve or sensor, is usually worth repairing. A boiler with a cracked heat exchanger, repeated leaks from the block or a long record of costly repairs is a replacement candidate. Ask the technician for the failed part, the cause and whether it is likely to fail again.

Boiler replacement involves new piping, venting and sometimes a different fuel setup. Our boiler repair service handles the plumbing side of diagnosis, and larger changes fall under plumbing installation. Get any replacement quote in writing, with the equipment, labor and permit costs separated.

Questions to ask before you approve a repair

Good answers to these protect you from unneeded work.

  • What exactly failed, and can you show me the part?
  • Is the fault in the plumbing, the burner or the controls?
  • Will you test pressure and check for leaks as part of the visit?
  • Does this repair need a permit or an inspection?
  • What happens to the warranty on the part and the labor?
  • What would you replace next if this fails again?
