Basement waterproofing is the work of keeping groundwater and surface water out of the lowest level of a house, or of collecting it and sending it away before it reaches finished space. The cause is rarely one thing. Roof water, grading, a clogged drain line, high groundwater and a failed pipe can all end up as the same wet floor. If water is rising right now, see our sump pump service or call (732) 466-1098.

We start by finding the source, because a waterproofing job aimed at the wrong cause fails after the next storm. A plumber covers drainage, sump pits, pipes and drain connections. Some parts of the job belong to other trades, and this page says where.

Where basement water actually comes from

Identify the entry point before choosing a fix. Each source has a different cure.

Floor-wall joint

Water that seeps where the slab meets the wall is usually groundwater pushing upward, and an interior drain suits it.

Cracks in the wall

Cracks leak after heavy rain. Sealing them is masonry work, but the water behind them still needs a way out.

Window wells

Wells without drains fill with rain and spill over the sill.

Roof and downspouts

Roof water dumped next to the foundation soaks the soil and finds the nearest gap.

Plumbing sources

A leaking supply pipe, a failing drain or a water heater tank can mimic a wall leak.

Hydrostatic pressure, in plain words

When the soil around a foundation stays saturated, the water presses on the walls and slab with real force. It will push through cracks, joints and porous concrete over time. That pressure, not the rain itself, is why a basement can stay damp for days after a storm has passed.

Relieving that pressure is the point of interior drains and sump systems. A drain along the footing collects water and sends it to a pit, from which a pump discharges it outdoors. A damp basement with no pit and no drain is a basement with nowhere for that pressure to go.

Fix the outside water first

Many basements dry out after these simple checks, and they cost far less than interior digging.

  • Clean gutters and make sure each downspout empties several feet from the foundation.
  • Check that underground downspout lines flow. Pour a bucket of water in the pop-up emitter and watch the outlet.
  • Walk the yard and note spots where soil slopes toward the house.
  • Clear leaves from window well drains and covers.
  • Look for sunken areas along the wall that hold water.
  • Run the hose beside the foundation for a few minutes and check inside for seepage.

A clogged underground gutter line is a plumbing job. We camera and jet it, which is covered in drain cleaning and hydro jetting.

Flood zones, Sandy and the township's rules

Neptune's flood maps use AE, AO, VE and X categories, and large residential areas, including all of North Island, sit in flood zones. After Superstorm Sandy, the state recovery report identified Shark River Hills, Ocean Grove and North Island as the most damaged and flood-susceptible neighborhoods. Three township pump stations were damaged and later made watertight.

If your address is mapped, check zone and rules before digging. The township requires a Floodplain Development Permit before construction or development begins. Permit rules vary, so ask the Neptune Township construction office. Interior drains in a flood zone also need a plan for what happens when surge outruns any pump.

When the water is from the sewer, not the ground

Water that rises at a floor drain and smells foul is not groundwater. It is a sewer or drain backup, and drying the walls will not fix it. Clean-looking seepage along walls after rain is groundwater. Telling them apart changes the repair.

Sewer backups are covered under sewer line cleaning. After Sandy, damaged pump stations risked raw sewage backing up into flooded streets, a reminder that flood zones can stress both systems at once.

What waterproofing cannot do

No system is magic. A sump pump without power does nothing, a drain line that clogs overflows, and a house in a surge zone can flood regardless of the pit. We will not promise a perfectly dry basement. We will explain which risks the work reduces and which it cannot.
