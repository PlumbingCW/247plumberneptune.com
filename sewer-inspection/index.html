A sewer inspection is a video look inside the line that carries wastewater away from your house. A camera on a flexible cable travels from the cleanout toward the street, recording cracks, offsets, roots, low spots and buildup that no one can see from above ground. Homeowners in Neptune book one before buying, after repeat backups, or before spending on repairs.

We show you the footage as it plays, point out what each defect means, and give a written note of what we found. The price is clear before the camera goes in.

What the camera shows

Belly

A sag that holds standing water and waste, which causes repeat clogs even in clean pipe.

Offset

Two sections that have shifted apart, leaving a lip that catches paper and lets roots in.

Root intrusion

Fine or thick roots entering at joints, a sign that a joint is open.

Cracks and fractures

Hairline cracks or full breaks, often with soil visible behind them.

Scale and grease

Rough deposits that narrow the pipe and can usually be cleaned off.

Collapse

A pipe wall that has caved in, which ends the inspection and begins the repair conversation.

Inside the visit, from cleanout to footage

How long it takes depends on the line, its length and what has to be cleared first.

  1. Find access

    We look for the main cleanout, or remove a toilet if there is none.

  2. Clear debris if needed

    A line full of sludge blocks the lens, so a short cleaning may come first.

  3. Run the camera

    The cable feeds toward the street while the screen shows the pipe and a footage counter.

  4. Locate with the sonde

    A transmitter in the camera head lets us mark depth and position on the ground.

  5. Review together

    We replay the clips with you and note anything that needs action.

Buying or selling: timing the scope

Book the inspection inside your attorney review or inspection window, so the findings can still shape the deal. A buyer who learns about a failing line after closing owns the repair. A seller who scopes first can fix a small problem on their own timeline or disclose it with footage.

Ask for the video, not only a summary. A second plumber or a lining contractor can review the same clips and give a repair opinion without another visit.

If the footage shows a defect, you have three choices: ask the seller to repair it, ask for a credit, or walk away. Having the pipe on video makes each of those conversations concrete instead of a debate over opinions.

Neptune has older housing, including Ocean Grove Victorian wood-frame homes dating from roughly 1870 to 1924, and newer townhomes and condos. Neighbors in Asbury Park with Victorian-era homes have the same question, and the same advice applies. Watching the footage costs less than learning about the pipe during a backup.

What a camera cannot do

Honest limits are part of the service, so here is what the footage does not tell you.

  • It cannot see inside walls, under slabs or in vent stacks above the roof.
  • It is not a pressure test, so it will not prove a supply line is leaking.
  • It will not pass a fully collapsed section or a blockage it cannot get around.
  • It cannot tell you who is responsible, the owner or the township.
  • It cannot measure soil conditions around the pipe.
  • A clear scan today does not promise the line stays clear in future.

After the scope: permits and cleaning

The inspection itself needs no permit. Any repair that follows may, and permit rules vary, so ask the Neptune Township construction office. If the footage shows only buildup, sewer line cleaning is usually the next step.
