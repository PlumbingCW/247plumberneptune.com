Commercial plumbing covers the water, drain, sewer and hot water systems of a business, from a restaurant kitchen or a retail strip to an office suite or a seasonal boardwalk shop. A plumbing failure there costs sales as well as repairs. If sewage is backing up, water is spreading or a line has burst, shut the main valve, stop using fixtures and call the 24/7 emergency line at (732) 466-1098.

Business plumbing differs from residential work in volume, schedules and who is responsible. Larger drains, grease, shared lines, tenant leases and inspection rules all play a part. This page explains how those pieces fit together and what we need from you to plan a visit.

Sewage on the floor or a main line down during business hours?

Close the main water valve if water is spreading. If sewage is rising from floor drains or toilets, stop using sinks, toilets and dishwashers, and keep customers and staff away from the wet area. Do not touch wet electrical equipment or the panel in standing water. If you smell gas, leave the building and call the gas utility or 911 before you call a plumber.

Restaurants and food service: grease, floor drains and disposals

Kitchens put heavy demands on drains, and most problems start with fat that cools and sticks.

Grease buildup

Fat hardens on pipe walls and narrows them until water slows. Regular cleaning keeps it from becoming a blockage.

Grease trap or interceptor

The trap catches grease before it enters the sewer. It needs scheduled pumping and cleaning, and a full one passes grease downstream.

Floor drains

A floor drain that gurgles or smells has a blocked line or a dry trap, so pouring water into it is the first check.

Disposals

A commercial disposal jams on bones and starch. Running cold water and keeping fat out helps.

Dish and prep sinks

Heavy use loosens fittings and wears supply lines, so small drips deserve attention.

Who pays: landlord or tenant?

The lease decides, not the plumbing. Many leases make the landlord responsible for the building's mains, risers and shared drains, and the tenant for fixtures, sink traps and anything inside the unit. A few shift more to the tenant. Read the clause before you call.

If the cause is not clear, call us first and tell the property manager at once. We write up where the failure was found on an itemized invoice, which helps sort out responsibility. Photograph the area and keep the leak or backup record for your insurer.

Opening and closing a seasonal shore business

Many shore businesses run seasonally. Nearby Belmar had 29.5% of its housing units vacant and Bradley Beach 33.0% in the 2020 figures, which shows how much of this coast sleeps in the off-season.

  • At closing, shut the main valve and drain supply lines, the water heater and any outdoor spigots.
  • Pour plumbing-safe antifreeze into traps where the building will be unheated.
  • At opening, open valves slowly and check every fixture and exposed joint for leaks.
  • Flush each line and run hot water to confirm the heater fires and holds temperature.
  • Test floor drains and the grease trap before the first service day.
  • Schedule backflow testing before inspection deadlines, not on opening week.

A short pre-season walk-through catches freeze damage while there is still time to fix it.

Backflow prevention, testing and hot water sizing

Backflow preventers keep dirty water from flowing back into the public supply. Many commercial properties are required to have them tested on a recurring basis. The water supplier and local officials set what applies, so ask which rules cover your building before the next test is due.

Commercial hot water is sized to peak use, not daily average. A restaurant with dishwashing and a salon with several chairs need more recovery than a small office. If the heater is undersized, staff run out in the rush. Replacement options are covered under water heater replacement, and the NJ plumbing subcode defines hot water as 120 to 140 degrees F.

Scheduling work around your business and your property manager

For non-emergency work, a little planning avoids lost sales and tenant complaints.

  1. Tell us the contact chain

    Give us the manager's name, the on-site contact and anyone who controls building access.

  2. Choose the window

    Many jobs fit before opening or after closing. We confirm which shutoffs affect neighbors.

  3. Get approval for shared systems

    Work on risers, shared drains or the main usually needs the owner's sign-off.

  4. Notify tenants

    Written notice of water shutoffs prevents surprise outages.

  5. Close out with paperwork

    You receive an itemized invoice with the cause and fix for your records.

Permits and inspections for commercial work

Permit rules vary, so ask the Neptune Township construction office what applies to your job. The township's 2018 fee schedule lists $27 per plumbing fixture or device including water heaters up to 99 gallons, $65 for a water heater of 100 gallons and up, and a $95 minimum per subcode. Confirm current fees with the office.

Plumbing work is governed by the National Standard Plumbing Code, NJ edition. The township's inspection stages, including rough and final, are requested at least 24 hours ahead. Belmar's Construction Department fines unpermitted work between $500 and $2,000, so check your town's rules before starting.
